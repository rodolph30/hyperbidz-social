# Auction lessons: the queue

One lesson a day. The daily run takes the **first lesson with `status: todo`**. Facts come from `research/auction_facts.md` (item codes like L4, S2, W9) and must be re-checked against the source links before they go on screen. Lessons teach how auctions work, why they are convenient, and (from lesson 10) which everyday buying and selling problems they solve; no HyperBidz, no app, no blame (see `LESSONS_SERIES.md`).

Format per lesson: number · title · status · pace (quick or standard, see `LESSONS_SERIES.md`) · music style · who (from lesson 10) · research items · the idea (lessons 01–09: hook → lesson → story → takeaway; from 10: problem → reframe → fix → takeaway) · quiz idea (optional story sticker).

**Owner, 23 Oct 2026: create the need.** From lesson 10, every lesson targets a real buyer or seller problem, changes how people see it with one sourced fact, and shows the auction fix (problem → reframe → fix → takeaway; read "The strategy" in `LESSONS_SERIES.md` and research items P1–P18). Each entry below gives the problem, the reframe, the fix and a takeaway line; the hook ideas are suggestions, the facts must be re-checked. **Who** says whose problem it is: Sami (seller), Nour (buyer), both, or a perspective lesson that changes what the word "auction" means.

**Owner, 18 Oct: interesting auction stories, not generic explainers.** Every lesson leads with a surprising story or fact (why, where, when, who, a record); a mechanism is only told through a real story. Reframe any "how X works" idea below that way before scripting it.

These are **viral animated informative shorts** (owner, 16 Oct), made in **style v3** since lesson 03 (owner's reference reel, 18 Oct; see `LESSONS_SERIES.md`): every lesson follows the viral checklist in `LESSONS_SERIES.md`. Pace: most are **quick** (20–35 s, one fact, snappy); a few that need steps are **standard** (40–55 s). A quick lesson must still be easy to follow: one idea per screen, nothing overwhelming.

### 02 · The day Rome was sold · status: made (17 Oct 2026) · pace: quick · music: marimba
Items: W3 (+ W1). Hook: "In 193 AD, someone bought the Roman Empire at an auction." Teach the ascending (English) auction: bids go up, the last bid wins, everyone sees the competition. Story: the Praetorian Guard auctioned the throne; Didius Julianus won with 25,000 sesterces per soldier, ruled 66 days. Takeaway: the word auction comes from Latin *augere*, "to increase".
Quiz: "In an English auction, who wins?" (the highest, last bid).

### 03 · The busiest minute on Wall Street · status: made (18 Oct 2026, first lesson in style v3, made in the owner's chat) · pace: standard · music: ambient
Items: S2, S1. Hook: "The busiest minute on the New York Stock Exchange is an auction." Teach: the closing auction gathers all buy and sell orders and finds one price that matches the most shares; about 9% of US volume trades there (check the latest figure). Takeaway: stock prices are discovered by bidding, every single day.
Quiz: "How does the stock market set its closing price?" (a closing auction).

### 04 · Why things end up at auction in Lebanon (was: How a Lebanese court auction works) · status: made (18 Oct 2026) · pace: standard · music: pluck

### 10 · The first number wins · status: made (v2, 25 Oct 2026, after the owner's "I didn't get a thing" on v1 of 24 Oct: one story, one fact) · pace: quick · music: lofi · who: Sami
Items: P1. **Problem:** you post your car; the first message offers half (show it as a number, e.g. "$4,500?" on an illustrative $9,000 car; never "last price"). **Reframe:** in negotiation experiments, whoever names the first number gets the better deal, and the first offer strongly predicts the final price (Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001; over half the variance); even real-estate agents were pulled by the list price and denied it (Northcraft & Neale 1987). A lowball isn't rude, it's an anchor. **Fix:** in an auction the buyers name the numbers, and each one is higher than the last. **Takeaway:** "In a chat, the first number pulls the price down. In an auction, every number pushes it up." Hook idea: "That lowball offer? Science says it just set your price."
Quiz: "In a negotiation, what strongly predicts the final price?" (the first offer).

### 11 · Paying a stranger first · status: made (26 Oct 2026; one story, one fact: the 8.1% reputation experiment) · pace: quick · music: keys · who: Nour
Items: P7, P6. **Problem:** Nour finds a phone online; the seller wants the money first. **Reframe:** US consumers reported losing $12.5 billion to fraud in 2024, and online shopping was the second most reported category (FTC); in Lebanon the ISF arrested a man in July 2026 who took payment for appliances on social media and never delivered (no names, no photo). Then the twist: trust can be measured. In a controlled eBay experiment, buyers paid 8.1% more to the same dealer when he sold under his established name (Resnick et al. 2006). **Fix:** a public track record and reviews, and money on the line from both sides. Never "safe" or "guaranteed". **Takeaway:** "Trust isn't a feeling. It's a track record you can check."
Quiz: "How much more did buyers pay the seller with a track record?" (8.1%).

### 12 · "Is it still available?" · status: made (27 Oct 2026; one story, one fact: the deadline effect, 41% / 53%) · pace: quick · music: marimba · who: Sami
Items: P3. **Problem:** Sami's inbox: "Is it still available?", "I'll think about it", "Can you hold it till next week?" Days pass, nobody decides. **Reframe:** in bargaining experiments with a 9-minute clock on screen, 41% of deals came in the last 30 seconds, and over half of those in the last 5 seconds (Roth, Murnighan & Schoumaker 1988). People don't decide when they're ready; they decide when time runs out. **Fix:** a countdown everyone can see. **Takeaway:** "No deadline, no decision."
Quiz: "When did most late deals happen in the experiments?" (in the last seconds before the deadline).

### 13 · The last-second loss · status: made (28 Oct 2026; one story, one study: 89 / 29 of 240 vs just one) · pace: quick · music: ambient · who: Nour
Items: P4. **Problem:** Nour leads an online auction for three days, and someone outbids her one second before the end. No time to answer. **Reframe:** in a study of 240 eBay auctions with a fixed end time, 89 had bids in the last minute and 29 in the last ten seconds; Amazon's auctions, which added time after every late bid, got just one bid in the last minute (Roth & Ockenfels 2002). The last-second win wasn't skill, it was the rule. **Fix:** a late bid adds time, so every bid can be answered. Never say "snipe". **Takeaway:** "A fair finish gives every bid an answer."
Quiz: "What stops last-second bids from winning unanswered?" (a rule that adds time after a late bid).

### 14 · Australia sells its homes on Saturdays · status: made (29 Oct 2026; updated to the newest Cotality week: 1,882 homes, a quiet week vs 3,236; most on a Saturday per SQM Research; under half sold) · pace: standard · music: pluck · who: perspective
Items: P8. **Perspective:** in Lebanon, "auction" sounds like trouble: courts, banks, seizures. In Australia it's how families sell their home: in one ordinary week this October, 1,749 homes went to auction across the capital cities, 703 in Melbourne and 632 in Sydney (Cotality). Show the open process: a set date, everyone bidding in the open, the highest bid wins. Don't claim they always sell (2026 clearance rates were under 50%). Any detail about front-yard auctions needs its own source first. **Takeaway:** "Here, 'auction' sounds like trouble. There, it sounds like Saturday."
Quiz: "In Australia's big cities, what do people often sell by auction?" (their homes).

### 15 · One more buyer · status: made (30 Oct 2026; one story, one fact: Bulow & Klemperer 1996, one extra bidder beats the best negotiation) · pace: quick · music: lofi · who: Sami
Items: P2. **Problem:** Sami sells to one buyer at a time; every chat starts from zero and he gives a little more each time. **Reframe:** two economists proved that a simple auction with just one extra bidder earns more than the best possible negotiation, and "the value of negotiating skill is small relative to the value of additional competition" (Bulow & Klemperer 1996). **Fix:** put everyone who wants it in one place, at one time. **Takeaway:** "One more buyer beats the best negotiator. Let them bid."
Quiz: "According to Bulow and Klemperer, what's worth more than negotiating skill?" (one extra bidder).

### 16 · Afraid of overpaying? · status: todo · pace: quick · music: keys · who: Nour
Items: P12 (+ callback to lesson 07). **Problem:** Nour avoids auctions: "I'll get carried away and pay too much." **Reframe:** in an open auction you don't pay what you're willing to pay. Bidders stop when the price passes their limit, so the winner pays about what the runner-up was willing to pay, plus one step (Vickrey; Lucking-Reiley 2000). **Fix:** decide your maximum before it starts (lesson 07) and let the others stop first. **Takeaway:** "You pay one step above the last person who wanted it."
Quiz: "At an open auction, the winner usually pays about…" (one step above the second-highest bidder's limit).

### 17 · The no-show · status: todo · pace: quick · music: marimba · who: Sami
Items: P5, L1. **Problem:** "I'm on my way." Sami waits an hour. Nobody comes. **Reframe:** one booking platform found that bookings with a deposit had a 1.7% no-show rate, and fully prepaid ones 0.9% (Tock, Dec 2023–Mar 2024). Lebanese law knew this already: to bid at a court auction you first deposit the full opening price, and losers get it back (CCP Art. 973). **Fix:** a small amount held when you bid, returned if you're outbid (never state an amount). **Takeaway:** "Serious buyers don't mind a deposit. Time-wasters do."
Quiz: "In a Lebanese court auction, what happens to a losing bidder's deposit?" (it's returned).

### 18 · Japan's used cars · status: todo · pace: quick · music: ambient · who: perspective
Items: P11. **Perspective:** "auctions are for paintings, or for seized things." In Japan, about 8 million used cars were put up at dealer auctions in a year; USS alone handled a record 3.5 million, and its Tokyo site took more than 20,000 cars in a single auction this spring (USS, FY2025). A country famous for reliable cars prices its used ones by bidding, every week. Say "dealers". **Takeaway:** "When millions of cars need a fair price, a whole country uses auctions."
Quiz: "How do Japan's car dealers trade used cars?" (at auctions).

### 19 · Afraid it sells for nothing? · status: todo · pace: quick · music: pluck · who: Sami
Items: P10. **Problem:** Sami's biggest fear: "What if one person bids $10 and takes my car?" **Reframe + fix:** the reserve: "the lowest price a seller is prepared to accept"; if bidding doesn't reach it, it doesn't sell, and the top bidder only gets the first chance to negotiate (Consumer Affairs Victoria). An auction isn't "any price": the seller sets the floor, the buyers set the rest. **Takeaway:** "A reserve is a floor, not a ceiling."
Quiz: "What's a reserve price?" (the lowest price the seller will accept).

### 20 · The haggling tax · status: todo · pace: quick · music: lofi · who: both
Items: P13. **Problem:** haggling: awkward, slow, and it feels like a fight. **Reframe:** across five studies, "95% of individuals choose not to negotiate up to 51% of the time"; people only thought haggling worth it for savings of 21–36%; and businesses can raise prices 5–11% and more than half of people will pay it (Hunsaker, Zhang & Lee, 2025). Haggling has a price, and both sides pay it. **Fix:** open bidding replaces the back-and-forth with the same rules for everyone. **Takeaway:** "No haggling. Just bids."
Quiz: "How much extra will many people pay to avoid haggling?" (about 5–11%).

### 21 · "DM for price" · status: todo · pace: quick · music: keys · who: Nour
Items: P9. **Problem:** Nour asks the price and gets "DM", then a different number than her friend got. **Reframe:** since this month in Melbourne, a home auction can't go ahead unless the seller's minimum price has been public for 7 days, and the sold price must be published afterwards; the minister said the aim is to end buyers' frustration at learning "the house was never in their price range" (Victoria, from 1 Oct 2026). Hidden prices aren't a tactic; they're a cost. **Fix:** every bid in the open: everyone sees the same number at the same time. **Takeaway:** "When the price is public, nobody gets a special price."
Quiz: "What must Melbourne sellers publish 7 days before an auction?" (their reserve price).

### 26 · Start low, end high · status: todo · pace: quick · music: keys · who: Sami
Items: P17 (+ P10). **Problem:** Sami thinks a low starting price means a low final price. **Reframe:** six studies found that in auctions, lower starting prices led to higher final prices; on real eBay data for 179 Persian rugs and 87 Nikon cameras, the low starts drew more bidders and ended higher (Ku, Galinsky & Murnighan 2006). It only works with people watching. **Fix:** start low, protect the floor with a reserve (lesson 19). **Takeaway:** "In a chat, start high. In an auction, start low and let them bid."
Quiz: "In the eBay study, what did lower starting prices lead to?" (more bidders and higher final prices).
